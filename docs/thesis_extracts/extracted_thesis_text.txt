HOW TO USE THIS THESIS FRAMEWORK

This document is your complete writing blueprint for the PhD thesis. It specifies every chapter, every major section, and every sub-section that the thesis must contain, together with: (a) the purpose and argument each section must advance; (b) which research objective(s) each chapter serves; (c) the methods and data each section draws on; (d) the expected word count range; and (e) the specific output or conclusion the section must deliver. Use it as a master checklist — tick each section as it is drafted, revised, and confirmed by your supervisor.
The thesis is structured across seven (7) chapters and is expected to run between 80,000 and 100,000 words in its final form. The framework is designed around three structural principles: (1) narrative coherence — every chapter must begin by connecting back to the research problem and end by connecting forward to the next chapter; (2) objective traceability — every empirical finding must be traceable to a specific research objective and research question; and (3) framework centrality — all chapters before Chapter 6 build toward the SMMS framework, and all chapters after Chapter 6 evaluate and contextualise it.

KEY CONVENTION: Sections shown with a blue sidebar are methodological or analytical in nature. Sections with a red sidebar highlight research gaps or critical arguments. Sections with a green sidebar indicate expected outputs or deliverables.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
Linked to: All Research Objectives (Contextual Grounding)  |  Est. 5,000–7,000 words

Chapter 1 serves as the intellectual foundation of the entire thesis. It does four things: introduces the reader to the research territory; establishes why Tanzania's infrastructure maintenance situation constitutes a genuine and urgent problem; states precisely what the research seeks to achieve; and maps the structure of the thesis. Every claim made in Chapter 1 must be supportable — this is not a literature review, but every fact about Tanzania's infrastructure challenges must be referenced. The chapter should be written with confidence and conviction; the reader must finish it believing both that the problem is real and that this research is the right response.

1.1  Background to the Study
Purpose: Establish the national infrastructure investment context that makes this research necessary. Move from the macro (national development plans) to the micro (maintenance management failures).
Begin with Tanzania's infrastructure development trajectory: FYDP I and II, BRN initiative, specific major projects (SGR, JNHPP, TANESCO grid, Dar port). Quantify the investment scale (USD billions). Establish that these are long-term service obligations, not just construction projects. Transition to the maintenance question: what happens to these assets after completion? Introduce the contrast between the scale of investment and the state of maintenance management.
Cover: national development context, infrastructure sub-sectors (roads, energy, railway, buildings), investment scale, relevance to national goals.
Do NOT: turn this into a literature review. Keep citations to key national policy documents, World Bank/AfDB reports, and one or two academic references on infrastructure sustainability.
End the section by signalling the 'maintenance gap' — the transition to the problem statement.
Expected Output: A crisp, evidence-based account of why Tanzania's infrastructure portfolio makes maintenance management a national priority — approximately 600–800 words.
1.2  Statement of the Problem
Purpose: Define the research problem with precision across three interlocking dimensions: data, decision-making, and framework. Each dimension must be named and explained.
This section is the argumentative core of Chapter 1. Articulate the three-dimensional problem: (1) the data problem — manual, episodic, subjective condition monitoring that produces latency, inaccuracy, and incompleteness; (2) the decision-making problem — absence of AI-driven analytics means maintenance decisions are reactive, intuitive, and unoptimised; (3) the framework problem — no validated, contextually appropriate framework integrating the full data-to-decision pipeline exists for Tanzania or Sub-Saharan Africa.
State explicitly: 'The central problem addressed by this research is...'
Connect each dimension to evidence: cite TANROADS's CMMS limitations, documented infrastructure deterioration statistics, absence of SMMS frameworks in African literature.
Show that the three dimensions are interconnected — solving only one does not solve the problem.
End with the 'so what' — consequences for sustainability, cost, and development outcomes if the problem is not addressed.
Expected Output: A precisely structured, three-dimensional problem statement that creates logical necessity for the research — approximately 700–900 words.
1.3  Research Aim and Objectives
Purpose: State the research aim and five specific objectives clearly and concisely. These are the contract between the researcher and the examiner.
State the overarching aim first. Then present the five specific objectives in alphabetical labelling (a–e), ensuring each objective is: active (starts with a verb — explore, establish, develop, identify, validate); specific; measurable; and directly traceable to a dimension of the research problem. The objectives must collectively and completely address the problem. Present the objectives as a numbered/lettered list, not embedded in prose.
Objective (a): Explore and document current practice — addresses the data and institutional dimension.
Objective (b): Establish key SMMS parameters — addresses the framework dimension.
Objective (c): Identify IoT sensors and develop CNN model — addresses the data dimension technically.
Objective (d): Establish intelligent decision-making parameters — addresses the decision-making dimension.
Objective (e): Develop and validate the SMMS framework — the integrating output objective.
Expected Output: A clear, unambiguous statement of aim and five objectives, each linked to a problem dimension — approximately 300–400 words.
1.4  Research Questions and Hypotheses
Present the five research questions, each corresponding to a specific objective. Then present the three research hypotheses (H1, H2, H3). For each hypothesis, state the null form as well, to show that the research design is capable of falsification. This establishes scientific rigour.
Expected Output: Five research questions and three testable hypotheses with null counterparts — approximately 300–400 words.
1.5  Significance of the Study
Purpose: Make the case for why this research matters — at national, institutional, knowledge, technological, and policy levels. Be concise but compelling.
Organise significance at five levels: (1) National development — asset value preservation, economic consequences of maintenance failure; (2) Institutional — upgrade pathway for TANROADS, TANESCO, TRC; (3) Knowledge — first validated SMMS framework for Sub-Saharan Africa, addressing five literature gaps; (4) Technological — open-source CNN model for concrete infrastructure in tropical conditions; (5) Policy — intellectual foundation for a national 4IR-in-maintenance policy framework.
Expected Output: A focused, multi-level significance statement — approximately 400–600 words.
1.6  Scope and Delimitations of the Study
State clearly what the research includes and excludes, and why. Cover: geographic scope (Tanzania; primary fieldwork in DSM, Dodoma, Arusha, Mwanza); infrastructure type (physical built infrastructure — roads, bridges, buildings, civil structures; not operational systems); maintenance modality (predictive maintenance only, not corrective or preventive); structural material (deep learning model scoped to concrete structures); institutional focus (public infrastructure agencies and their stakeholders).
Distinguish between delimitations (deliberate scope choices) and limitations (unavoidable constraints). Limitations belong in Section 1.7, not here.
Expected Output: A precise scope statement that protects the research from overreach without making it trivially narrow — approximately 300–400 words.
1.7  Limitations of the Study
Acknowledge anticipated limitations honestly: data access constraints in public agencies; IoT network limitations in some regions; the newness of the field meaning limited directly comparable prior studies; model training data availability; generalisation limits of a single-country study. For each limitation, state the mitigation strategy employed.
1.8  Organisation of the Thesis
Provide a chapter-by-chapter overview (one paragraph per chapter, 3–5 sentences each) explaining what each chapter covers and how it connects to the one before and after it. This gives the reader a navigational map of the whole thesis.
Expected Output: A concise roadmap paragraph for each of the seven chapters — approximately 400–500 words.


CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW
Linked to: Objective (a) — boundaries of practice | All objectives (theoretical grounding)  |  Est. 18,000–22,000 words

Chapter 2 is the intellectual backbone of the thesis. Its purpose is not to describe everything ever written about SMMS — it is to build the case for your research. It must do four things simultaneously: (1) demonstrate your mastery of the relevant field; (2) identify the specific gaps that your research fills; (3) establish the theoretical constructs and relationships that will underpin your framework; and (4) justify your methodological choices. Every sub-section should end with a critical evaluative statement, not just a descriptive summary. The literature review must be structured to build toward the conceptual framework in Section 2.10, which is the logical product of everything reviewed.

2.1  Introduction to the Chapter
State the purpose and organisation of the chapter. Explain the search strategy: databases used (Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore), search terms (smart maintenance, IoT infrastructure, predictive maintenance, BIM GIS integration, developing country construction, etc.), year range, and inclusion/exclusion criteria. A PRISMA-style flow diagram of the search process may be included as a figure.
2.2  Maintenance Management: A Generational Perspective
Introduce the four-generation maintenance evolution model (Moubray, 1997; Kumar & Galar, 2018) as the conceptual lens for the entire chapter. Map Tanzania's current practice (Generation 1–2) and establish that Generation 4 (Maintenance 4.0) is the target. This framing provides the narrative structure for the entire literature review and justifies why IoT, AI, BIM, and GIS are all necessary — they are the enabling technologies of Generation 4.
2.2.1  Generation 1: Reactive / Breakdown Maintenance
2.2.2  Generation 2: Preventive / Scheduled Maintenance
2.2.3  Generation 3: Condition-Based Maintenance
2.2.4  Generation 4: Maintenance 4.0 / Smart Maintenance
Critical Argument: The generational transition in high-income countries took 50+ years of incremental development. Tanzania cannot afford this timeline. The SMMS framework must therefore be designed as a deliberate leapfrog strategy that manages both the technology adoption and the institutional transition simultaneously — a requirement almost entirely absent from existing frameworks.
2.3  Smart Maintenance and Industry 4.0
Establish the theoretical link between Industry 4.0, Construction 4.0, and Maintenance 4.0. Define each term precisely and explain how they relate hierarchically. Review the core enabling technologies of Industry 4.0 as they apply to maintenance management.

There is a critical shortage of validated CNN models for civil infrastructure — particularly concrete structures in tropical climatic conditions where deterioration patterns differ substantially from temperate contexts.

This section synthesises the integration dimension — not individual technologies, but how they work together. Review the evidence on BIM–GIS, IoT–BIM, IoT–GIS, and full-stack IoT–BIM–GIS–AR/VR integration architectures.

2.9  Infrastructure Maintenance Management in Developing Countries
This section is critical to the Tanzania focus. Review what is known about maintenance management systems, challenges, and outcomes specifically in developing and Sub-Saharan African contexts. This section establishes the 'developing country dimension' as a legitimate and distinct research domain, not merely a subset of global practice.

The five gaps identified above collectively define the space this research occupies.

Cover: ISM methodology and its application in construction management; TISM as an interpretive extension of ISM; Design Science Research (DSR) and its application to framework and system development in engineering and information systems research; mixed-methods approaches in smart maintenance research.

End with the chapter's 'argument statement': a paragraph that distils what the literature review has established and what it has left unresolved, setting up the research design and the conceptual framework.

CHAPTER 3: RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
Linked to: All Objectives (methodological justification)  |  Est. 10,000–13,000 words

Chapter 3 is the 'how' chapter — it explains and justifies every decision made about how the research was conducted. The cardinal rule is: every methodological choice must be justified, not just described. Do not say 'a questionnaire was used'; say 'a structured questionnaire was used because [reason], which is appropriate for this study because [alignment with objectives].' The chapter must demonstrate that the research design is coherent, rigorous, and capable of generating the evidence needed to answer the research questions and test the hypotheses. Write this chapter as if a highly sceptical examiner is reading it who will challenge every choice.

3.1  Introduction
State the purpose of the chapter. Provide a one-paragraph overview of the research design. Introduce the research 'onion' (Saunders et al., 2009) as the organisational framework for this chapter — philosophy → approach → strategy → methods → time horizon → data collection.

3.2.1  Ontological Position
3.2.2  Epistemological Stance
3.2.3  Pragmatism as the Guiding Philosophy — Justification
Justify pragmatism as the philosophy of this study: it values practical outcomes, legitimises mixed methods, and is suited to problem-solving research. Contrast briefly with positivism and interpretivism to show why pragmatism is the superior fit for an applied, framework-development study.

3.3.1  Mixed-Methods Design — Rationale
3.3.2  Applied Research Justification
3.3.3  Design Science Research (DSR) as the Parent Methodology for Framework Development
This is a critical section. Explain DSR's six-activity cycle (Hevner et al., 2004; Peffers et al., 2007) and map each DSR activity to a specific phase or output of this research. Show how DSR 'wraps around' the conventional social science methods (questionnaire, interview, Delphi) and provides the framework for turning empirical findings into a validated artefact. Include a mapping table: DSR Activity vs. Research Phase vs. Methods vs. Output.
3.3.4  Three-Phase Research Design
Present the three-phase design (Phase 1: Investigation; Phase 2: Model Development and Parameter Validation; Phase 3: Framework Development and Validation) as a structured narrative. Include a research design diagram (describable as a text figure).
3.4  Population and Sampling

Principle: Each method must be justified in relation to specific research questions/objectives. Show how the methods are complementary and how triangulation is achieved.
3.5.1  Systematic Literature Review — Protocol and Search Strategy
3.5.2  Structured Questionnaire Survey — Design, Piloting, Distribution
Explain questionnaire design: Likert-scale construction; pilot testing; validity and reliability checks (Cronbach's Alpha for internal consistency); stratification by sector; distribution method. Include a table showing how each questionnaire section maps to a specific objective.
3.5.3  Semi-Structured Expert Interviews — Protocol and Conduct
Interview schedule design; purposive selection of interviewees; procedures for scheduling, recording (with consent), transcription, and member-checking. Explain why semi-structured format is preferred over structured or unstructured for this context.
3.5.4  Two-Round Delphi Expert Study — Design and Administration
Explain Delphi round structure: Round 1 (open-ended, generates parameter list); Round 2 (rates importance, seeks convergence). Consensus criterion (IQR ≤ 1 or ≥ 75% agreement). Expert selection criteria and panel composition. Address Delphi limitations and mitigations.

Data collection: structural health monitoring datasets (public and locally collected). Python 3.12 / TensorFlow / Keras. CNN architecture design and hyperparameter selection. Training, validation, and test split (e.g. 70/15/15). Performance metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, AUC-ROC. Cross-validation strategy.

Coding procedure (open, axial, selective); theme development; inter-rater reliability check; member-checking for validity.
3.6.2  Quantitative Analysis — Relative Importance Index (RII) and Descriptive Statistics
RII formula and interpretation; ranking methodology; frequency and mean score analysis; appropriate inferential tests (chi-square, Kruskal–Wallis for non-parametric comparisons across groups).

CHAPTER 4: CURRENT INFRASTRUCTURE MAINTENANCE MANAGEMENT PRACTICE IN TANZANIA
Linked to: Objective (a) — Exploring current practice and its boundaries  |  Est. 12,000–15,000 words

Chapter 4 is the first empirical chapter. Its purpose is to answer Research Question 1: What is the current state of infrastructure maintenance management practice in Tanzania — what methods are used, what factors drive or constrain it, and what are its boundaries and limitations? The chapter draws on data from the questionnaire survey, semi-structured interviews, literature review, and document analysis. It produces the empirical baseline — the 'Generation 1–2 reality' — against which the SMMS framework in Chapter 6 is positioned. Data must be presented first, then analysed, then interpreted. Use figures, tables, and charts throughout to present survey data visually.

4.1  Introduction
State the chapter's purpose, link it to Objective (a) and RQ1, and outline the chapter structure.
4.2  Profile of Research Participants
Present demographic and professional profile data for all survey and interview participants: sector distribution (TANROADS, TANESCO, TRC, consultants, etc.); years of experience; job roles; educational qualifications. Use bar charts and pie charts. This establishes the credibility and representativeness of the sample.
4.3  Current Maintenance Methods and Practices
4.3.1  Maintenance Approach Adopted (Reactive vs. Preventive vs. Condition-Based)
Present questionnaire data on the maintenance approaches currently used. Tabulate by infrastructure type (roads, bridges, buildings, energy) and by institution. Use RII to rank the prevalence of different approaches.
4.3.2  Data Collection and Condition Assessment Practices
How is condition data currently collected? What tools, frequencies, and processes are used? What is the role of manual inspection vs. technology? Present interview evidence alongside survey data.
4.3.3  Existing Computerised Maintenance Management Systems (CMMS)
Document which CMMS tools are in use (TANBRIDGEMAN, PTMS, others). What are their capabilities and limitations? What percentage of institutions use them? How effective are they judged to be by practitioners?
4.3.4  Maintenance Decision-Making Processes
How are maintenance decisions made? Who makes them? What information do they draw on? How are priorities set? Identify the decision chain from field inspector to budget allocation.
4.4  Key Factors Influencing Maintenance Management Practice
4.4.1  Enabling Factors (Drivers of Good Practice)
4.4.2  Constraining Factors (Barriers to Effective Maintenance)
Use RII to rank both drivers and barriers. Present the top 10 drivers and top 10 barriers. Compare rankings across institution types (government agency vs. private consultant). Use interview evidence to provide depth on the most significant factors.
4.5  Technology Readiness and Digital Literacy
4.5.1  Awareness of IoT, AI, and Smart Maintenance Technologies
4.5.2  Existing Technology Infrastructure
4.5.3  Digital Skills Assessment Among Maintenance Professionals
This section is critical for the SMMS framework design — it establishes the institutional capacity starting point.
4.6  IoT Network Coverage Assessment
4.6.1  4G/LTE Coverage for Infrastructure Maintenance Corridors
4.6.2  NB-IoT and LoRaWAN Availability
4.6.3  Coverage Gaps and Implications for SMMS Deployment
Present the network assessment findings using maps and tables by region. This answers RQ4 on network readiness and directly informs the IoT architecture choices in the SMMS framework.
4.7  Comparative Analysis: Tanzania vs. Regional/Global Practice
How does Tanzania's current maintenance practice compare to other Sub-Saharan African countries and to global Maintenance 4.0 standards? Position Tanzania on the maintenance generation scale using the framework from Chapter 2. This comparative perspective strengthens the motivation for the SMMS framework.
4.8  Discussion and Synthesis
Integrate survey, interview, and documentary evidence to answer RQ1 comprehensively. What do the findings collectively reveal about the state of maintenance management in Tanzania? How do they confirm, challenge, or extend the picture painted in the literature review? What are the most critical gaps that the SMMS framework must address?
End with a transition paragraph that connects the findings of Chapter 4 directly to the parameter investigation in Chapter 5: 'The findings of this chapter establish that... These limitations create the specific requirements that the SMMS parameters in Chapter 5 must address, particularly...'
Expected Output: An empirically grounded, analytically rich baseline profile of Tanzania's infrastructure maintenance management practice, answering RQ1 completely — approximately 12,000–15,000 words.


CHAPTER 5: KEY SMMS PARAMETERS, DECISION-MAKING SYSTEM, AND DEEP LEARNING PREDICTIVE MAINTENANCE MODEL
Linked to: Objectives (b), (c), (d)  |  Est. 16,000–20,000 words

THESIS WRITING GUIDANCE — PROCESS AND QUALITY STANDARDS

Writing Process Guidance
Do not write the thesis in chapter order. The recommended writing sequence is: (1) Chapter 3 (Methodology) first — it forces clarity about the research design before data collection; (2) Chapter 4 (Empirical findings — current practice) after fieldwork Phase 1; (3) Chapter 5 (Empirical findings — parameters and model) after Phase 2; (4) Chapter 2 (Literature review) — update the draft written for the proposal, now incorporating findings; (5) Chapter 6 (Framework) after all analysis is complete; (6) Chapter 7 (Discussion and conclusions) after all other chapters are drafted; (7) Chapter 1 (Introduction) last of the chapters — it is the easiest to write once everything else is done; (8) Preliminary pages absolutely last.
Do not try to write perfectly on the first draft. Write freely, then revise. Submit each chapter draft to your supervisor for feedback before moving on. Keep a writing log and set weekly word count targets — 1,000 words per working day is a reasonable sustained pace during full-time writing phases.
Referencing Standards
Use APA 7th Edition throughout. No exceptions.
Use Mendeley, Zotero, or Endnote from Day 1 to manage all references.
Never cite a source you have not read in full (at minimum the relevant section).
Secondary citations (citing a source as cited in another) are permissible but should be minimised — always seek the primary source.
Every figure and table must have a numbered caption and a source attribution.
Figure and Table Standards
All figures must be high-resolution (minimum 300 dpi for printing).
Framework diagrams (especially Figure 6.X — the SMMS framework) should be professionally drafted; consider using Lucidchart, draw.io, or Visio.
All tables should be formatted consistently: header row in dark colour with white bold text; alternating row shading; cell borders; consistent font.
Statistical tables should follow APA reporting format.
Language and Academic Writing Standards
Write in formal academic English. Third-person passive is acceptable but not mandatory — the University of Dar es Salaam accepts first-person active where appropriate.
Avoid colloquial language, contractions, and informal expressions.
Define every technical term and acronym on first use.
Every paragraph should have a clear topic sentence, supporting evidence, and a linking sentence.
Avoid plagiarism absolutely — all text must be original or properly cited. Use Turnitin to check similarity before submission.
